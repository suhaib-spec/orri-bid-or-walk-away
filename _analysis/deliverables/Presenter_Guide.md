# Part 1: The story in plain English

### The situation

It is **mid-July 2018** (we pretend). **BP** is selling a bundle of **19 royalty interests** on oil and gas fields in the **Gulf of Mexico**, the ocean south of Texas and Louisiana. The fields sit around **9 "platforms"**, meaning 9 separate fields or production sites.

BP issued the bid instructions on **July 9** and wants bids by **August 15, 2018**. That gives us **5 weeks** to decide: **bid or walk away?** If we bid, how much, and where does the money come from?

### What exactly is being sold?

An **overriding royalty interest (ORRI)** is a right to receive **a small percentage of the money from every barrel of oil and every unit of gas** sold from a field.

- It is like owning a few cents of every dollar a lemonade stand makes, **without paying for the lemons, the cups or the stand.**
- The royalty owner **pays no drilling costs, no operating costs, and no clean-up costs** at the end of the field's life.
- The royalty owner also **has no control**. The oil companies that run the fields (the **operators**) decide when to drill, shut down or repair.

So we would be buying **a stream of future checks** whose size depends on three things:
1. **How much oil and gas comes out** (production).
2. **The price of oil and gas.**
3. **What percentage we actually own**, which is trickier than it looks.

### What did our team find?

**Finding 1: Three fields are almost all the value.**
**Kodiak, Lucius and Stones** are about **95%** of the value. **Kodiak alone is about half, and Kodiak is one single well.** If that well breaks, a big part of the value disappears. That is our biggest risk.

**Finding 2: The fine print shrinks the package.**
We read the legal documents for every royalty. Several look bigger on the seller's list than they really are:
- **Lucius** looks like 2% + 2% = 4%. But the leases joined neighbours in a shared "unit", so the real share is **1.43%** of the field. That is about 2.8 times smaller.
- **Pompano** looks worth about **$71 million** if you match it to a field with the same name in the industry report. But our royalty covers **a different area** that has produced **nothing since 2014**. Real value: about **$0**.
- **Ship Shoal** is listed at 8%. We think it is really **4%**, because the original owner could only give away a royalty from its own half.
- **Stones** covers 8 blocks, but **only 1 block** produces.
- **Kodiak** appears twice on the list, but it is **one** royalty. Counting it twice would double it.

**Finding 3: The seller's 2017 revenue is real, but 2017 is not a guide to the future.**
BP said the package earned **$20.4 million in 2017**. We rebuilt that number from **government production records** and got **$20.6 million**, so BP's number is honest. But 2017 was unusual:
- Kodiak was **offline for 8 months**.
- Lucius changed how it shares production.
- Stones **shut down** in early 2018.

So we forecast from the **2018 monthly data**.

**Finding 4: The seller's forecast is too optimistic.**
The data room includes forecasts from **Wood Mackenzie (WoodMac)**, a well-known research firm. We compared their 2018 forecast with **what actually came out of the ground** in January to May 2018. Most fields were **well below forecast**. The gap is mostly **future drilling** that hasn't happened, and the case says **we cannot assume when drilling will happen**.

### What is it worth?

We valued **only the wells already producing**. That gives about **$102 million** in today's money (base case). In our optimistic **"operator-trend" case**, where declines are slower and closer to the seller's WoodMac profiles, it is about **$134 million**.

### What do we bid, and why is it clever?

We bid **$98 million cash**, plus **up to $24 million more** in "performance payments". We pay those only if **public government production reports** show that:
- Kodiak keeps producing above set levels in 2019, 2020 and 2021, with oil at **$55 or more** (**$5M each year**);
- Kodiak gets a **second well** (**$5M**);
- Stones gets a **new well** (**$4M**).

**Why this wins:**
- **BP** sees a headline of up to **$122M**. If BP believes its own forecast, it expects to collect most of it.
- **We** only pay extra if the extra oil really shows up. If BP's forecast is wrong, we paid $98M and still earn about **15% a year**.
- Nobody argues about whether a trigger was met, because the measure is **public government data**.

If BP insists on one fixed cash price, our **maximum is $105M**. Above that we walk away.

### Where does the money come from?

The case warns that **the bank said no**, because offshore is risky with hurricanes. It also says loans take **2 to 3 months**, and we have 5 weeks.

Our plan **doesn't need a bank to close**:
- On closing day we need about **$84M**, not $98M. The deal is backdated to January 1, 2018, so BP hands us the ~$17.5M of royalties it already collected for January to June.
- **95%** comes from an **existing royalty investment fund** that has already raised its money (~**$79M**). Our team puts in **5%** (~**$4M**).
- The fund can decide in weeks because **our research is already done** and the asset needs **no drilling money**.
- If the fund **hasn't signed by August 8**, we **don't bid**. We never make a bid we can't pay for.
- **After** closing, we can take an optional **bank loan of $25–30M** at **7.25%**. That is the **5.00% prime rate** plus 2.25%. The deal doesn't depend on it.

### What could go wrong?

1. **The Kodiak well fails.** This is the biggest risk: about **−$21M** of value, and a slightly negative return.
2. **Oil falls to $45:** about **−$18M**. We protect ourselves by **hedging**, which means locking in prices for 2019–20.
3. **Lucius is still paid the old way:** about **−$7M**.
4. **Hurricanes:** storms shut production, so we cut the forecast by **2% a year**.
5. **The Kodiak lease's paperwork after its long shutdown.** We require proof before closing.

### What do other bidders see?

- Some will believe the brochure, overpay, then fail to raise the money.
- Some will multiply the 2017 revenue by 5–7 and miss the 2018 changes.
- Disciplined royalty funds are our real competition.

We bring **checked ownership, public data and a pay-on-proof price**.

### How did we use AI?

AI read 60+ legal PDFs, pulled government data, drafted the model and role-played tough reviewers. **AI made real mistakes**: it said Lucius was 4% and that Pompano was worth $71M. We caught them by checking the original documents. All decisions were made by the team.

# Part 2: Glossary (plain English)

### Oil and gas basics

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Gulf of Mexico (GoM)** | The ocean south of Texas and Louisiana; a major US oil region. |
| **Offshore** | In the ocean. More expensive and riskier (storms). |
| **OCS (Outer Continental Shelf)** | Federal waters, farther out than state waters. The federal government owns the seabed, so there is **no state severance tax**. |
| **Shelf** | Shallow water near the coast. Older, smaller fields (Grand Isle, Main Pass, Ship Shoal). |
| **Deepwater** | Water deeper than about 1,000 feet. Bigger, newer fields (Kodiak, Lucius, Stones, South Santa Cruz, Orion, Pompano). |
| **Upstream** | The oil-production part of the industry (finding and producing oil), as opposed to refining and selling it. |
| **Field** | An underground area holding oil and gas. One field can span several blocks. |
| **Well** | A hole drilled into the field to bring oil and gas up. |
| **Completion** | Finishing a well so it can produce. A "new completion" is a new producing well or zone. |
| **Recompletion / zone** | Re-working a well to produce from a different underground layer (a "zone"). |
| **Platform / spar / FPSO** | The structure at sea that processes the oil. A **spar** is a tall floating cylinder. An **FPSO** is a ship that processes and stores oil. "9 platforms" means 9 separate fields or sites. |
| **Subsea tieback / host** | A well on the seabed piped to a nearby platform (the **host**). Kodiak is tied back to the **Devils Tower** spar. If the host shuts down, Kodiak stops. |
| **Barrel (bbl)** | Standard unit of oil: 42 US gallons. |
| **kb/d or thousand bbl/day** | Thousand barrels per day; how fast a field produces. |
| **mcf** | Thousand cubic feet, the unit for natural gas. |
| **Gross field oil** | All oil from the field, before anyone's share is taken out. Slide 5 uses this. |
| **Royalty-burdened oil** | The oil our royalty applies to (for Kodiak, only oil from below 17,500 ft). |
| **Production** | How much oil and gas comes out. |
| **Decline rate** | Wells produce less each year. A **35% decline** means next year's output is about 35% lower. |
| **Uptime** | Share of time a well is actually running. |
| **Shut in** | Production stopped (for repairs, storms or other problems). |
| **Phase 2** | A planned second round of drilling at a field (Stones Phase 2 = new wells). |
| **Capex** | Capital expenditure: money spent drilling wells and building facilities. Royalty owners pay none. |
| **Decommissioning / plugging** | Sealing wells and removing platforms at the end of a field's life. Very expensive. **Royalty owners do not pay it.** |

### Ownership and legal terms

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Lease** | Government permission to drill in an area. Federal offshore leases have numbers like **OCS-G 24102**. |
| **Block** | A square on the offshore map, about 3 × 3 miles, named by area and number (e.g. **MC 727**). |
| **Area codes** | Map regions: **MC** = Mississippi Canyon, **KC** = Keathley Canyon, **WR** = Walker Ridge, **GI** = Grand Isle, **MP** = Main Pass, **SS** = Ship Shoal. |
| **Lease continuity** | Keeping a lease alive. After its first term, a lease must keep producing (or working on wells). If production stops for more than **180 days** without a government-approved **suspension**, the lease can end, and our royalty with it. |
| **Working interest (WI)** | Ownership that **pays the costs** and gets the profits. The oil companies hold this. |
| **Operator** | The company that runs the field: Shell runs Stones, Anadarko runs Lucius, Deep Gulf Energy runs Kodiak. |
| **Royalty** | A cost-free share of production revenue. The government gets one, called the "lessor's royalty". |
| **Overriding royalty interest (ORRI)** | A royalty **carved out** of a working interest by a private party (here BP, or companies BP bought). Cost-free. **This is what we are buying.** |
| **Gross sales** | Sales revenue before any costs. Royalties are a percentage of this. |
| **Decimal** | The royalty written as a decimal (0.06375 = 6.375%). |
| **Attachment A** | The seller's list of the 19 interests and their decimals. |
| **Instrument** | A legal document (e.g. the one that created a royalty). |
| **Assignment** | A document transferring ownership. When BP sold a working interest, it often **reserved** (kept) an ORRI in the assignment. |
| **ABOS** | "Assignment and Bill of Sale", a type of transfer document. |
| **Serial register page** | The government's official record of who owns a lease. |
| **Vesting** | Proof that ownership legally passed (e.g. from Amoco to BP after their merger). |
| **Title** | Legal proof of ownership. "Title diligence" means checking the paperwork. |
| **Title defect** | A paperwork problem (missing document, smaller share). Our bid letter cuts the price if one is found. |
| **Depth limit** | The royalty applies only to oil from certain depths (Kodiak: below **17,500 ft**). |
| **Unit / unitization** | Neighbouring leases over one field combine into a **unit** and split production by agreed percentages. Lucius became a unit on **Jan 1, 2018**. |
| **Tract factor** | Each lease's agreed share of the unit. Lucius: KC 874 = **24.77%**, KC 875 = **46.67%**. |
| **Unit basis vs lease basis** | Unit basis: the royalty is paid on the lease's share of the whole unit. Lease basis: the old way, paid only on oil from wells physically on that lease. |
| **Contraction** | A unit shrinking to drop leases that don't contribute. This could happen to Kodiak's MC 771 and the 7 non-producing Stones blocks. |
| **Payor** | The company that sends the royalty check. There are **22 payors** here. |
| **Remittances / division orders** | A remittance is a payment statement. A division order tells the payor what share to pay to whom. |
| **Suspense** | Money a payor is holding back, often over a paperwork question. |
| **True-up** | A catch-up payment correcting earlier underpayments (e.g. Lucius, after the unit was backdated to January). |
| **Royalty relief** | A 1990s federal program that let some deepwater leases skip the government royalty on early production. The Stones royalty is calculated "in the same manner" as the government royalty, so it might be paused too. |
| **Severance tax** | A state tax on oil produced. None applies in federal offshore (OCS) waters. |
| **30 CFR 250.1701** | The federal rule listing who pays for decommissioning: lessees and operating-rights owners, **not** royalty owners. |

### Government and data sources

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **BOEM** | Bureau of Ocean Energy Management, the federal agency that manages offshore leases. It **publishes monthly production for every lease**, free. This is our main data source. |
| **BSEE** | Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement, BOEM's sister agency (safety, unit approvals). |
| **OGOR** | Oil and Gas Operations Report, the monthly production report operators must file. BOEM publishes it. |
| **WoodMac (Wood Mackenzie)** | A private industry research firm. The seller included its field reports and forecasts. Useful, but optimistic. |
| **NYMEX strip / strip prices** | Futures prices for oil and gas for each future month, from the New York Mercantile Exchange, as of one day. We used **Aug 15, 2018**: about **$60–64** a barrel for 2019–20. |
| **WTI** | West Texas Intermediate, the benchmark US oil price. |
| **Prime rate** | The base interest rate US banks charge their best customers: **5.00%** on Aug 15, 2018. |

### Money and valuation terms

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Cash flow** | Money coming in each month or year. |
| **Discount rate** | How much less we value money that arrives later, because of waiting and risk. At 12%, $112 next year is worth about $100 today. The case says use **10–20%**; we chose **12%**. |
| **Present value (PV)** | All future cash flows converted to today's dollars. "PV @12%" means at a 12% discount rate. |
| **NPV (net present value)** | PV minus the price paid. Positive = a good deal at that rate. Ours is about **+$4M** at $98M. |
| **IRR (internal rate of return)** | The yearly return the investment earns, like an interest rate. Ours is **~15%** in the base case. **Link to PV:** at a 15% discount rate the wells are worth **$98M**, exactly our price. That is what "a 15% return at $98M" means. |
| **Unlevered / levered** | Unlevered = return **without** borrowed money (all our headline returns). Levered = **with** a loan, which can raise the investor's return but adds risk. |
| **MOIC / "1.3x"** | Multiple on invested capital: cash back ÷ cash paid. 1.3x = $1.30 back for each $1. |
| **Payback** | The year cash received equals cash paid: **2022** in the base case. |
| **Profit (undiscounted)** | Total cash received minus price, ignoring timing. **~$31M** base case; **~$70M** optimistic, after paying BP $15M. |
| **Base case** | Our main, cautious forecast built from public monthly data. |
| **Operator-trend case** | Our **optimistic** case: declines closer to the seller's WoodMac profiles (Kodiak 22%, Lucius 25%) and no downtime. It is *not* a forecast the operators gave us. |
| **Blended view** | A probability-weighted mix of our two cases: **60% base, 40% operator trend**. |
| **Risked drilling upside** | Possible new wells, with their value reduced for the chance they never happen: **+$13M**. **Not** in our price. |
| **Run-rate** | What the asset earns at its current pace, not a past year's total. |
| **Hurricane haircut** | Cutting forecast production by **2% a year** for storm shutdowns. |
| **Deductions ($3/bbl, $0.40/mcf)** | Transport and processing costs some payors subtract before paying royalties. This is our **assumption**. |

### Deal and funding terms

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Bid / sealed bid** | Our offer price. Other bidders can't see it. |
| **Effective date (Jan 1, 2018)** | The deal is backdated: the buyer is entitled to royalties from Jan 1, 2018. |
| **Closing (Sept 14, 2018)** | The day we pay and become owner. |
| **Closing credit** | BP already collected Jan–Jun 2018 royalties (**~$17.5M**) and subtracts them from what we pay. Royalties arrive about **60 days** after the oil is sold, which is why only Jan–Jun is credited by closing. Royalties for July to Sept 14 (**~$8.8M**) reach us at **final settlement**, around January 2019. |
| **Award** | BP picks the winning bid (Aug 30, 2018). |
| **Deposit** | 10% of the price (**$9.8M**). We **assume** it is wired at award (market practice, not in BP's instructions). It comes from the fund and counts toward the $98M. |
| **Performance payments / earn-out / contingent payment** | Extra money paid later **only if** something happens. Common in oil deals. |
| **Price test** | A performance payment is due only if oil (WTI) averages **$55 or more** that year. |
| **Reversion** | If we miss a performance payment, **part of the Kodiak royalty goes back to BP**. This **protects** BP if we fail to pay. |
| **Ceiling / walk-away price** | The most we'd ever pay: **$105M**. |
| **Allocation** | The bid instructions require splitting the price across all 19 interests (Excel "Allocation" tab). |
| **Equity** | Investors' own money (not borrowed). |
| **Royalty fund / anchor fund** | An investment fund that already raised money to buy royalties. "Anchor" = the main investor. |
| **Co-invest / promote** | **Co-invest:** our team puts in its own money (5%). **Promote:** an extra share of profits the deal team earns only if returns beat the investors' hurdle. |
| **Hurdle rate** | The minimum return investors require (our fund: **12–15%**). |
| **Term sheet** | A short list of key deal terms, agreed before the full contract. |
| **NDA** | Non-disclosure agreement. We need BP's consent to share the data room with our funders. |
| **Investment committee (IC)** | The fund's decision-making group. |
| **Reserve letter** | An independent engineer's report on how much oil is left and what it's worth. Funds and banks require one. |
| **Commitment letter** | The fund's signed promise of money. **This is our go/no-go gate.** |
| **Critical path** | The steps that would delay closing if they ran late. The bank is **not** on it. |
| **Reserve-based loan (RBL)** | A bank loan secured by the oil still in the ground, usually up to ~50% of its value. |
| **Hedge** | Locking in a future oil price now with a financial contract, so a price crash hurts less. |
| **Sources & uses** | Where the money comes from and where it goes. The two must be equal. |

### Analysis terms

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sensitivity / tornado chart** | Change one assumption at a time and see how much value moves. The sorted bars look like a tornado. |
| **Monte Carlo simulation** | Running the model thousands of times (here **6,000**) with random but realistic inputs, including a **6% chance each year that the Kodiak well fails**, to see the range of outcomes. |
| **Middle (P50)** | Half of the outcomes are above it, half below. Ours: **~$98M**. |
| **"1-in-10 below"** | Only 10% of outcomes are worse than this: **~$79M**. |

| **Red-team review** | Deliberately attacking your own plan. We had AI play critics. |
| **Hindsight** | Using information not available on Aug 15, 2018. **Not allowed.** |

## A. The decision and the price

**Q1. Why bid at all? The case practically invites walking away.** *(Slide 1)*
At $98M the producing wells alone earn about 15%, above our 12% cost of money, and the risky parts are paid only on proof. Walking away throws away a package we've already verified.

It's a little below our $102M base value at 12%, which gives about a 15% return and a ~$4M cushion. It also matches the middle of our 6,000 simulations, which include the chance Kodiak fails.

**Q3. Where does the $105M ceiling come from?** *(Slide 1)*
Base value $102M plus a quarter of the $13M risked drilling upside, so about $105M. We'll share a little drilling value with BP, never all of it. Above $105M our base case drops to about 10%, below our 12% cost of money.

**Q4. At $105M your base case earns ~10%, below your own 12% rate. Why ever pay it?** *(Slide 8)*
We wouldn't on the base case alone. A blended view (60% base, 40% optimistic) gives about 16–17% at $105M. That's why $105M is a ceiling, not our bid.

**Q5. Where does the $24M come from?** *(Slide 8)*
Three Kodiak production tranches of $5M each ($15M), $5M for a second Kodiak well, and $4M for a new Stones well.

**Q6. How did you set the Kodiak triggers (11.0 / 8.5 / 6.5 thousand barrels a day)?** *(Slide 8)*
They sit between our two forecasts. Our base case predicts about 9.5 / 6.6 / 4.8, so no payment. The optimistic case predicts about 13.1 / 10.3 / 8.0, so all three are paid. If BP's story is right, BP gets paid; if ours is right, we don't overpay.

**Q7. Why would BP accept performance payments when it asked for all-cash?** *(Slides 1, 8)*
They're cash payments, just later and conditional. BP sees up to $122M, and if it believes its own forecast it expects to collect them. If BP refuses, we offer a plain $105M all-cash alternative. Payments are protected: if we miss one, part of the Kodiak royalty goes back to BP. Earn-outs are common in oil deals; in April 2018, Fieldwood bought Noble's deepwater assets with a $100M contingent payment.

**Q8. Isn't $98M a lowball? What's your chance of winning?** *(Slide 10)*
It's below brochure prices, but it's what a funded buyer can actually pay. Brochure bidders may bid higher and then fail to fund. Our headline is up to $122M, and we can actually close. **Don't give a percentage chance of winning; we have no evidence for one.**

**Q9. Why does the fixed $105M earn 27% in the optimistic case but yours only 26%?** *(Slide 8)*
In that case we pay $98M + $15M = $113M, more than $105M. That's our insurance premium: about 1 point less in the best case and 3–4 points more in each bad case.

**Q10. Your simulation's middle ($98M) equals your price. So it's a coin flip whether you earn 12%?** *(Slide 7)*
On producing wells alone, roughly yes, and that already includes the chance Kodiak fails. On top of that we get new drilling we haven't paid for: about $13M of risked value, of which we pay at most $9M and only if it happens. That's exactly why we stop at $98M.

The base case assumes Kodiak keeps running at 90% uptime. The simulation adds a 6% chance each year that the well fails, plus random swings in decline and price. So $98M is closer to an expected value, and that's why we bid $98M, not $102M.

**Q53. What value might others overlook?** *(Slide 10)*
The Lucius catch-up payments (about $2–3M) and the right to audit past payments. The earn-out structure itself is also value others miss, because it bridges what BP believes and what the data shows.

**Q54. How did you allocate $98M across 19 interests?** *(Excel "Allocation" tab)*
In proportion to each interest's producing value, with a token $0.1M on non-producing ones (Pompano and the 7 idle Stones blocks). **Q55. Why not bid on just Kodiak, Lucius and Stones?** *(Slide 2)*
BP strongly prefers one buyer for the whole package, and the six small interests add only about $6M. Leaving them out would rank us lower for little saving.
